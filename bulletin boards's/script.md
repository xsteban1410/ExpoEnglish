# LINUX — Presentation script (10–15 min, easy English)

Each `##` section has an id in brackets. The voice (`Expotition Voice/voice.toml`) uses the same ids.
Board = the poster board (pliego) where that part is written.

---

## [intro] Welcome — Board 1
Good morning, everyone. Today we are going to talk about Linux.
Maybe you think you have never used Linux. But I promise you: you use it every day.
By the end of this presentation, you will know what Linux is, why there are so many versions of it,
how it works, and where you can find it. And at the end, you can do a short quiz on your phone
to find out which Linux you are.

## [what] What is Linux? — Board 1
Linux is an operating system, like Windows or macOS.
An operating system is the program that controls the computer. It connects the hardware,
like the keyboard, the screen and the memory, with the programs you use.

## [kernel] The kernel — Board 1
The heart of Linux is called the kernel.
The kernel is the part that talks directly to the hardware. It decides which program can use
the processor, the memory and the disk, and when.
Strictly speaking, the word "Linux" means only the kernel. When we add tools and programs to it,
we get a complete operating system.

## [history] A short history — Board 1
In 1991, a Finnish student called Linus Torvalds started Linux as a hobby.
He wrote a message on the internet: it was "just a hobby, it won't be big".
He was wrong! Today, thousands of people and companies work on it.
Linux also uses many tools from the GNU project, started by Richard Stallman in 1983.
That is why some people say "GNU/Linux".
The mascot of Linux is a penguin. His name is Tux.

## [opensource] Free and open source — Board 1
Linux is free and open source.
Free means two things: you don't have to pay for it, and you have the freedom to use it,
study it, change it and share it.
Open source means that the code is public. Anyone can read it and improve it.

## [distros] What is a distro? — Board 2
Because Linux is open source, anyone can take the kernel, add programs, a design and tools,
and create their own version. This version is called a distribution, or a "distro".
There are hundreds of distros! It is like ice cream: the base is the same, but there are many flavours.

## [families] Distro families — Board 2
Most distros belong to a few big families.
The Debian family: Debian, Ubuntu and Linux Mint. They are stable and very good for beginners.
The Red Hat family: Fedora and Red Hat Enterprise Linux. They are modern and popular in companies.
The Arch family: Arch Linux, Manjaro and CachyOS. They are fast, always up to date, and for people
who like to control everything.
There are also special distros, like Kali Linux for security, or SteamOS for video games.

## [layers] How Linux works — Board 3
We can imagine Linux as layers, like a cake.
At the bottom is the hardware: the physical parts of the computer.
On top of the hardware is the kernel.
On top of the kernel is the shell, and the programs and services of the system.
And at the top are the applications: the browser, the music player, the games.
When you click on something, your message goes down through the layers, and the answer comes back up.

## [desktop] Desktop and terminal — Board 3
You can use Linux in two ways.
The first way is with a desktop environment, with windows, icons and a mouse, like Windows.
The most popular desktops are GNOME and KDE Plasma.
The second way is with the terminal. The terminal is a window where you write commands.
It looks difficult, but it is very fast and powerful.

## [commands] Five basic commands — Board 3
Here are five basic commands.
"ls" shows the files in a folder.
"cd" changes the folder.
"pwd" tells you where you are.
"mkdir" creates a new folder.
And "sudo" lets you do things as the administrator. Be careful with sudo!

## [everywhere] Linux is everywhere — Board 4
Now, where is Linux? Everywhere!
Most of the servers on the internet use Linux: Google, Facebook, Netflix, Wikipedia.
Android, the system on most phones in the world, uses the Linux kernel.
The five hundred most powerful supercomputers in the world all use Linux.
You can also find it in smart TVs, cars, routers, and even on Mars, in a NASA helicopter.

## [why] Why use Linux? — Board 4
So, why use Linux?
One: it is free.
Two: it is secure. It has fewer viruses, and updates are fast.
Three: it is customizable. You can change almost everything.
Four: it gives a second life to old computers.
And five: it has a big, friendly community that helps you.

## [outro] Conclusion and quiz — Board 4
To finish: Linux is a free operating system, made by people all around the world,
and it is in your phone, your TV and the internet.
Now it's your turn! Scan the QR code on the cards and answer the quiz "What Linux are you?".
Thank you very much for listening. Any questions?
